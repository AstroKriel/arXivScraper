abstract: Intermittency is a fundamental property of astrophysical turbulence because
  it concentrates strong gradients, dissipation, and transport into rare, localized
  structures. In the multiphase interstellar medium (ISM), turbulent driving is especially
  important because the balance between compressive and solenoidal motions can regulate
  how extreme fluctuations are distributed among thermal phases. Yet conventional
  increment statistics quantify intermittency without identifying the structures that
  host it. We analyze three matched ideal-MHD simulations of a thermally multiphase
  neutral medium that differ only in the compressive fraction of the driving. Density,
  velocity, and magnetic-field increments are non-Gaussian in every thermal phase.
  Increasing compressive driving redistributes density and magnetic fluctuations preferentially
  into the cold gas rather than uniformly enhancing them across phases. We identify
  the structures associated with extreme increments using local, scale-matched diagnostics
  of compression, thermal gradients, vorticity, strain, magnetic curl, and magnetic
  tension. Extreme magnetic increments preferentially occur where magnetic curl and
  magnetic tension are simultaneously enhanced, more strongly than extreme velocity
  increments are associated with their kinematic hosts. Extreme velocity increments
  show comparable enrichment in vorticity and strain, whereas extreme density increments
  preferentially trace converging flows and thermal gradients only in the unstable
  medium. These associations weaken beyond a phase-dependent coherence scale. Our
  results show that turbulent driving controls how intermittency is partitioned among
  thermal phases, while distinct physical structures host extreme density, velocity,
  and magnetic fluctuations.
