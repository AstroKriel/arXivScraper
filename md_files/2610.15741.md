abstract: Measurements from the NASA Parker Solar Probe (PSP) mission demonstrate
  that inside $\sim20$ solar radii ( $\Rs$ ) the magnitude of the interplanetary magnetic
  field does not fall off simply as $1/R^2$ , with $\avg{|B|}\,R^2$ 15--20\% below
  its value at $\sim60\,\Rs$ . Here we use PSP magnetic field measurements over $10.6$
  -- $56.8\,\Rs$ to show that the outward rise of $\avg{|B|}\,R^2$ is not due to the
  global structure of the heliospheric magnetic field or proximity to the heliospheric
  current sheet but is the direct result of the growth of large-amplitude, spherically
  polarized Alfvén waves (so-called `switchbacks). Over the same distance range, $\avg{B_R}\,R^2$
  stays constant, so that the sector-signed radial flux is conserved. The wave amplitude
  $ε= B_{\perp,{\rm rms}}/\avg{B_\parallel}$ grows from $0.26$ to $0.57$ as $R^{1/2}\cosα$
  , and $\avg{|B|}/\avg{B_R}$ rises by $20\%$ . A simple model parametrized by the
  broadening of the distribution of deflection angles reproduces the effect to high
  accuracy. These results demonstrate that as the relative wave amplitudes grow with
  increasing solar distance, a dynamo-like process operates to enhance the field magnitude
  by up to $20\%$ . By enhancing magnetic pressure this has implications for the large
  scale solar wind structure in the low-beta inner heliosphere, as well as partially
  resolving the long-standing discrepancy between remote measurements of the solar
  magnetic field and those made in situ by earlier missions farther from the Sun.
