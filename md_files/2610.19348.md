abstract: While magnetic flux ropes (MFRs) are widely accepted to exist prior to many
  solar eruptions, the physical mechanism responsible for triggering and subsequently
  driving their eruptions remains debated. Classical models commonly attribute eruption
  onset to ideal magnetohydrodynamic instabilities, particularly the torus instability,
  while magnetic reconnection is often regarded as a secondary process. Using high-resolution
  three-dimensional MHD simulations, we investigate the eruption of an MFR that forms
  self-consistently through flux cancellation driven by converging motions and photospheric
  diffusion following an initial shearing phase. The pre-eruption MFR rises quasi-statically
  and reaches a height where the decay index exceeds the classical torus instability
  threshold, yet the system continues to evolve quasi-statically without eruption.
  Instead, eruption begins only when the current sheet beneath the MFR becomes sufficiently
  thin for magnetic reconnection to set in. Increasing numerical resolution systematically
  delays the eruption onset, whereas the decay index at eruption onset remains nearly
  unchanged, demonstrating that the onset is governed by reconnection rather than
  torus instability. During the eruption, magnetic reconnection continuously converts
  surrounding sheared arcades into newly reconnected twisted flux that merges with
  the pre-eruption MFR and continuously to build the erupting MFR. A component-resolved
  analysis of the Lorentz force work further shows that the newly reconnection flux
  contributes predominantly to the main acceleration phase. These results demonstrate
  that magnetic reconnection plays a central role throughout the entire lifecycle
  of the eruptive MFR... it builds the MFR, directly triggers its eruption, and provides
  the dominant driving of the eruption.
