abstract: We present a comprehensive Sun-to-Earth analysis of the extreme space weather
  event of 18 -19 January 2026, hereafter the Martin Luther King (MLK) storm, which
  produced a severe (G4) geomagnetic storm and one of the most intense solar radiation
  storms of Solar Cycle 25. The geomagnetic disturbance was driven by a fast Earth-directed
  coronal mass ejection (CME) associated with an X1.9-class flare from NOAA Active
  Region 14341. We combine multi-viewpoint remote-sensing observations, in situ measurements
  from Solar Orbiter and Wind, and physics-based magnetohydrodynamic simulations using
  the CORHEL-CME framework to reconstruct the eruption from the low corona to 1 au
  and identify the mechanisms governing its geoeffectiveness. Observations and simulations
  indicate that the eruption originated from a right-handed magnetic flux rope embedded
  in a complex magnetic environment dominated by a neighboring positive-polarity coronal
  hole. The simulations reproduce the observed CME morphology, asymmetric expansion,
  magnetic fields measured at both spacecraft, and Dst index evolution. The main discrepancy
  is attributed to filament material not included in the model. Favorable magnetic
  polarity alignment between the CME and coronal-hole field minimized magnetic erosion
  through interchange reconnection, enabling rapid escape and preservation of the
  strong southward magnetic field that drove the geomagnetic storm. The prolonged
  recovery phase resulted from interaction between the trailing CME and a following
  high-speed stream from the same coronal hole. These results demonstrate that storm
  intensity was primarily established by the coronal magnetic configuration, whereas
  storm duration was controlled by the ambient solar wind.
