abstract: Coronal mass ejections (CMEs) are major drivers of space weather, yet their
  thermodynamic evolution remains insufficiently constrained. This thesis develops
  a comprehensive framework linking CME kinematics, thermodynamics, internal force
  balance, and geoeffectiveness through analytical modelling and multi-viewpoint observations.
  The developed FRIS model uses observed kinematics to infer otherwise inaccessible
  internal plasma properties, including the polytropic index. A systematic investigation
  of fast CMEs reveals an initial heat-release phase followed by a transition towards
  near-isothermal evolution, typically around 3-9 solar radii. The evolving contributions
  of thermal-pressure and centrifugal forces, opposed by the Lorentz force, establish
  a connection between expansion and thermal evolution. The findings demonstrate the
  limitations of assuming a fixed polytropic index in magnetohydrodynamic models of
  CME propagation. At 1 au, in situ observations reveal persistent non-adiabatic evolution,
  accompanied by turbulence and localised intermittency. The interacting CMEs associated
  with the May 2024 great storm show restructured magnetic and thermal configurations,
  producing double-flux-rope-like signatures and species-dependent heating, with electron
  thermodynamics as a better indicator of recent interactions. Statistical analysis
  of nearly 600 ME demonstrates that 45% exhibit net heating with a solar cycle dependence.
  Notably, High-impact ejecta preferentially exhibit stronger heating signatures,
  establishing thermal state as a complementary diagnostic of geoeffectiveness. Overall,
  this thesis establishes CMEs as intrinsically non-adiabatic systems whose thermodynamic
  evolution is coupled to expansion, internal force balance, solar-cycle variability,
  and CME-CME interactions, with important implications for understanding geoeffectiveness
  and improving space-weather assessment.
